# Swimming programming

Read this reference when building or revising a swim session, swimming week, or
training block. The training-planning skill owns the athlete's overall schedule;
this reference supplies swimming-specific decisions.

Before programming, read the athlete profile, current swimming benchmarks,
recent completed sessions, available pool/course, time limit, equipment,
symptoms, and nearby running or lifting sessions. If material swimming capacity
is unknown, use a conservative assessment session rather than inventing pace or
volume.

## Choose one primary purpose

Each session should have one primary purpose:

- learn or stabilize a technical skill;
- build comfortable aerobic continuity;
- develop sustained controlled speed;
- practise fresh short speed;
- develop event-specific speed endurance or execution;
- recover while retaining water feel;
- collect a benchmark that will change the plan.

Secondary work should support rather than obscure that purpose. Do not make
every session simultaneously hard, technical, long, and test-like.

## Intensity

Use a 0–10 rating of perceived exertion alongside observed pace and technique:

| Element               |      Typical effort | Intended experience                                        |
| --------------------- | ------------------: | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Recovery or easy      |                 1–3 | Relaxed, comfortable breathing, little accumulating strain |
| Steady endurance      |                 3–5 | Sustainable rhythm with capacity beyond the planned repeat |
| Sustained harder work |                 6–8 | Demanding but controlled across the complete set           |
| Event-pace work       |            Variable | Specific speed and mechanics for the chosen event          |
| Short speed           | Usually 9–10 intent | Fast and coordinated with recovery before quality declines |

These are coaching ranges, not fixed physiological zones. Effort depends on task
duration. Do not transfer running heart-rate zones into swimming without
swimming-specific evidence, and do not use heart rate as the sole guide for
brief sprints.

## Technique programming

Give technique work a concrete question, such as whether the athlete can breathe
without disrupting the next catch. Use this sequence:

1. Observe ordinary swimming at the relevant effort.
2. Explain one change in plain language.
3. Use a short drill or constrained task when it clarifies that change.
4. Return immediately to ordinary swimming with the same cue.
5. Compare comfort, direction, rhythm, effort, and speed with the original task.
6. Keep, modify, or discard the cue based on the response.

Load [technique.md](technique.md) for diagnosis. Load [drills.md](drills.md) only
after selecting the problem being addressed. One or two drills for one problem
are usually more useful than a catalogue of unrelated drills.

Do not postpone all fitness work until technique is “perfect.” Build skill under
manageable demands, then gradually test whether it survives longer repeats or
higher speed.

## Endurance progression

Intervals often make continuity and technique easier to control than one long
swim. Progress one major variable at a time:

- add repetitions at the same distance, effort, and rest;
- use longer repetitions at the same total main-set distance;
- reduce rest slightly while holding pace and technique;
- increase pace modestly after the existing task is comfortable.

A change from 6 × 100 to 3 × 200 preserves distance but may substantially
increase difficulty. Observe the response. Use continuous swimming when it
serves the goal, assessment, or enjoyment; endurance does not mean swimming
without stopping at all costs.

Do not impose a universal percentage-based weekly increase or claim that one
progression rule prevents injury.

## Sustained work and CSS

Use a credible CSS estimate or observed repeat pace as an initial reference, then
calibrate it. A first sustained set may begin slightly slower than CSS with
enough recovery to remain controlled. Reduce repeat length or pace when the set
is clearly too difficult; adjust gradually when it is clearly too easy.

Exact offsets do not apply equally to every swimmer, stroke, and repeat length.
Do not build a precise-looking zone table from one estimate. Keep sustained
training distinct from all-out testing.

## Sprint and race-specific work

Put pure speed after an adequate warm-up and before substantial fatigue when
speed is the priority. Use brief efforts, small initial volume, normal breathing,
and enough recovery for high-quality movement. A competent swimmer may begin
with 4–8 short efforts, but actual distance, number, and recovery depend on their
background.

Lengthen recovery or stop the speed block when intended speed or coordination
repeatedly declines. A small percentage slowdown may be a review flag when
timing is reliable, but it is not a universal stopping threshold.

Distinguish:

- **Short speed:** Producing high speed with ample recovery.
- **Speed endurance:** Sustaining or repeating fast speed under accumulating
  fatigue.
- **Race execution:** Coordinating pace, walls, breathing, and finishing for an
  event.

A broken 100 is a training task. Adding its swim segments does not create an
achieved continuous 100 result.

## Weekly allocation

Use these as adaptable patterns only after confirming the athlete tolerates the
frequency:

| Available swims | Possible emphasis                                                                                |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1/week          | One focused technical theme plus manageable endurance; alternate small speed exposure when ready |
| 2/week          | One technique/steady session and one speed or sustained session                                  |
| 3/week          | Technique with short speed; steady endurance; controlled harder repeats                          |
| 4/week          | Technique/recovery; speed; endurance; sustained or event-specific work                           |

Do not cram every goal into every week. Most total distance can remain
comfortable even when sessions have distinct emphases.

For a multiweek block, first establish routine and a useful baseline, then repeat
and refine skills, progress either continuity or volume, introduce goal-specific
work when supported, and finish with a comparison only when it will be useful.
Do not force a test because a calendar says the block has ended.

## Session specification

Every prescribed session should state:

- goal and primary success criterion;
- assumed pool length and units;
- expected duration or total distance;
- warm-up and skill preparation;
- main work with repetitions, distance, stroke, effort or pace, and rest;
- one main technical cue when relevant;
- easy finish;
- an easier or shorter alternative;
- what to report afterward.

Use swimming notation precisely:

- “6 × 100 m, rest 20 s” means twenty seconds after each repeat.
- “6 × 100 m on 2:20” means begin a repeat every two minutes twenty seconds.
- “Build within each 50” means accelerate inside each repetition.
- “Descend 1–4” means each complete repetition becomes faster.
- “Negative split” means the second half is faster than the first.

Do not say “on 20 seconds” when “rest 20 seconds” is intended. Recalculate the
session total after shortening or replacing a block. See R09 in
[resources.md](resources.md) when pace-clock terminology needs external support.

## Equipment

Use equipment to answer a specific training question and then check transfer
without it:

| Tool                | Useful purpose                                           | Main limitation                                                       |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Short fins          | Support balance, rhythm, or brief skill practice         | Change speed, kick, and body position; assisted results stay separate |
| Front snorkel       | Reduce head-turning demand while observing another skill | Requires familiarization; never use it for breath restriction         |
| Pull buoy           | Compare body position or isolate some arm work           | Can hide balance problems and increase arm loading                    |
| Kickboard           | Make a kick task practical                               | Head-up position may strain the neck or back                          |
| Hand paddles        | Add feedback or resistance for experienced swimmers      | Introduce gradually and stop with pain; not a default beginner tool   |
| Tempo device        | Explore rhythm at a defined rate                         | State whether beeps mean entries or cycles; do not chase rate alone   |
| Watch or pace clock | Record time and trends                                   | Verify length-count and timing errors                                 |
| Camera              | Reveal movement that is difficult to feel                | Requires safe placement, permission, privacy, and useful views        |

Do not prescribe larger paddles to address shoulder pain. Avoid adding several
unfamiliar tools at once.

## Coordinate with running and lifting

Read the shared schedule before placing a demanding swim:

- Heavy overhead lifting, snatches, jerks, presses, and pulling may affect
  shoulder readiness for sprint swimming.
- Hard running and lower-body lifting may affect kick work, wall pushes, and
  general recovery.
- A technically difficult swim is not automatically recovery merely because
  swimming is low impact.
- Additional dryland work should answer an observed need and fit the existing
  programme.

Place priority technique or sprint work when the athlete is reasonably fresh.
When two sports share a day, decide which session matters most and use the
athlete's demonstrated response rather than a universal order.

## Adaptation

Before and during a session, distinguish ordinary effort, technical overload,
environmental constraints, and symptoms.

| Situation                             | Adjustment                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Poor sleep or unusual general fatigue | Reduce demand, simplify the set, or choose easy skill work                      |
| New pain or pain changing the stroke  | Stop the provoking task; do not use equipment or maximal effort as a workaround |
| Illness or concerning symptoms        | Defer strenuous work and seek appropriate care when needed                      |
| Long interruption                     | Re-establish comfortable volume and technique before hard benchmarks            |
| Crowded pool                          | Choose an executable task and defer precise speed or wall experiments           |
| Missing benchmark                     | Use conservative effort and short repeats while collecting evidence             |

For ordinary fatigue or skill overload, change one useful variable: slow down,
shorten the repeat, add rest, remove equipment, simplify the cue, or end the
demanding block. If a task is too easy, do not simultaneously increase volume
and speed while reducing rest.

Do not diagnose overtraining from one poor swim. Review several weeks, actual
conditions, adherence, symptoms, and other training before changing everything.

## Post-session use

Accept incomplete reports. Extract what is available:

- completed blocks and modifications;
- stroke, distance, and units;
- repeat times and actual rest or send-off;
- effort and relevant technique response;
- equipment;
- symptoms during, after, and—when useful—the following day;
- the athlete's free-text impressions.

Ask only for ambiguities that would materially change interpretation. Preserve
the actual session rather than silently rewriting it to match the plan. A
modified session may represent good judgment.

Update a benchmark only when the result meets its definition. A fast main-set
repeat may be a training best without being a race result or a rested time trial.

For ready-made starting structures, read [examples.md](examples.md) and adapt one
to the athlete; never copy a template without checking current capacity and
available time.
