# Swimming examples and quality checks

Read this reference only when a concrete session structure or an analogous
coaching decision would help. These are adjustable examples, not prescriptions.
Confirm the athlete's capacity, symptoms, course, available time, and nearby
training before using one.

All sample distances are metres in a 25 m pool. Adapt the design to the actual
course rather than relabelling yards as metres. Duration estimates are broad and
depend on the athlete, lane conditions, drill speed, and recovery.

## Session templates

### Technique with aerobic transfer — 1400 m

Suitable for a swimmer already comfortable with 100 m repeats and similar total
distance.

- 200 easy, then 4 × 50 progressively more purposeful with 15–20 seconds rest.
- 6 × 50 as 25 selected drill plus 25 ordinary swimming, with 20–30 seconds
  rest.
- 6 × 100 easy or steady with 20 seconds rest, retaining one cue.
- 100 easy.

Goal: retain one selected change during ordinary swimming. Record main-set times,
whether the cue survived the later repeats, effort, and symptoms. Easier version:
4 × 100 for the transfer block, or 50 m repeats when coordination deteriorates.

### Technique at speed — 1500 m

Suitable for a swimmer with consistent training and comfortable short fast
efforts.

- 300 easy, then 4 × 50 building gently.
- 4 × 25 progressive but not maximal, with 30–45 seconds rest.
- 4 × 50 as 25 relevant drill plus 25 ordinary stroke.
- 6 × 25 fast from a consistent push, with 2–3 minutes recovery or enough to
  preserve quality.
- 4 × 100 easy.
- 150 easy.

Goal: practise coordinated speed while fresh. Stop or extend recovery when
quality declines. A swimmer for whom 25 m is too long may swim a brief fast
segment and finish easily; record only the actual sprint segment as fast work.

### Controlled endurance — 1800 m

Suitable for a swimmer already comfortable with 200–300 m repeats.

- 300 easy.
- 4 × 50 as 25 drill plus 25 ordinary swimming, with 20 seconds rest.
- 3 × 300 steady with 30–45 seconds rest.
- 4 × 50 smooth ordinary swimming, checking the main cue.
- 200 easy.

Goal: hold a stable rhythm over longer repeats. Record useful splits and where
technique changes. An easier continuity version is 6 × 150 at the same total
main-set distance, with adequate rest; label it as a different task.

### Calibrated sustained work — 1600 m

Suitable for a swimmer with recent consistency and a useful repeat pace or
credible CSS estimate.

- 300 easy.
- 4 × 50 gradually building, with 20 seconds rest.
- 8 × 100 at a controlled target, with 20 seconds rest.
- 4 × 50 easy using one technical cue.
- 100 easy.

Goal: find a demanding repeatable pace without turning the set into eight time
trials. Begin conservatively near or slightly slower than a calibrated CSS when
appropriate. For a first exposure, 4–6 main repetitions may be sufficient.

### Developing swimmer — 500 m

Suitable for someone who can swim 25 m comfortably and return safely to support.
It is not a substitute for water-confidence lessons.

- 4 × 25 very easy, resting until ready.
- 4 × 25 with one simple whole-stroke cue.
- 6 × 25 easy, with enough rest to avoid rushed breathing.
- 4 × 25 alternating the cue and ordinary swimming.
- 2 × 25 easy.

Goal: repeat comfortable breathing and one simple skill. Progress by making the
same task more comfortable before adding a small amount of swimming.

### Four-stroke skill practice — 800 m

Suitable only when the athlete is comfortable with every included stroke.

- 200 comfortable warm-up in familiar strokes.
- 4 × 50, one selected drill-to-swim pair for each stroke.
- 8 × 25 easy practice, two per stroke.
- 4 × 25 using the most useful stroke and cue from the session.
- 100 easy.

Replace unfamiliar strokes with supervised learning tasks. For butterfly,
several successful strokes followed by easy freestyle can replace a full 25;
record stroke distances honestly.

## Limited time and crowded lanes

For a 30-minute session, retain a proportionate warm-up, the priority task, and
an easy finish. Remove secondary work. Do not reduce sprint recovery until a
speed session becomes an unintended fatigue set.

In a crowded lane, replace precise sprint timing with controlled builds or
steady repeats and defer wall experiments. Do not stop mid-lane to discuss a
drill or perform stationary work in traffic.

## Worked decisions

### New swimmer without times

If the athlete can swim one length but then needs a long rest and wants to reach
1000 m, clarify pool length, water competence, and whether breathing, fear,
effort, or symptoms caused the stop. Begin with manageable repeats or supervised
teaching. Do not prescribe a CSS test or immediate 1000 m time trial.

### Experienced swimmer with old records

Preserve an old personal best as historical. After a long interruption,
establish recent comfortable repeat capacity before prescribing current work
from the old performance.

### Technique before speed

If an athlete wants perfect technique before swimming fast, explain that the
skill eventually needs to work at goal speed. Establish control first, then add
brief faster transfers with ample recovery; do not require maximal efforts.

### Lower stroke count but worse result

If stroke count falls while the athlete becomes slower and more breathless,
check pace, effort, push-off distance, and breathing. Investigate overgliding or
excessive force rather than treating the lower count as proof of improvement.

### Pure speed requested under fatigue

If a swimmer requests pure speed after substantial hard swimming, explain that
fatigue may prevent high-quality sprint practice. Move the priority work earlier
next time. For the current session, choose a smaller controlled task or recovery
according to readiness.

### Equipment associated with pain

If paddles provoke shoulder pain, stop paddle work. Do not increase paddle size
as a strengthening strategy. Recommend appropriate assessment for persistent or
recurrent symptoms.

### Breath-restriction request

Do not provide a no-breath or hypoxic challenge. Offer short rested speed with
normal breathing and technique work that reduces disruption during the breath.
See R36 in [resources.md](resources.md) when an external safety source is useful.

## Final plan check

Before delivering a swim session or block, verify:

- It matches the athlete's demonstrated level in every included stroke.
- Benchmark date, units, course, start, equipment, and protocol are compatible
  with any prescribed pace.
- Estimated, derived, planned, and achieved values are labelled correctly.
- Distance totals and approximate duration are internally consistent.
- Rest and send-off are distinguished.
- The task can be executed safely in the stated pool and lane conditions.
- Every drill has a purpose and an ordinary-stroke transfer.
- Sprint work retains enough recovery and normal access to breathing.
- A practical easier version is included.
- Pain and concerning symptoms are handled rather than trained through.
- The session fits the other sports and recovery context.
- The athlete knows which small amount of evidence to report afterward.
