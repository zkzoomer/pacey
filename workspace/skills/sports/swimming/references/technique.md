## Technique assessment and feedback

### Use a consistent assessment order

First consider safety and symptoms, then breathing, body alignment, rhythm, propulsion, and finally event-specific refinements. This is a starting order, not a rule that every swimmer has the same main problem.

Ask what breaks first: breathing becomes uncomfortable, the legs sink, the arms tire, the rhythm stalls, or the swimmer loses speed. A description can guide a hypothesis but does not establish its cause.

Use this assessment record:

```text
Task and pace:
Observation or athlete report:
Evidence and view available:
Possible explanation:
Alternative explanation:
One cue or drill to try:
Success criterion during normal swimming:
Response:
Next action:
```

Do not diagnose a mobility restriction or muscle weakness from a single visual feature. A crossover entry could reflect recovery direction, breathing, balance, habit, or the camera angle.

### Self-assessment without video

Use questions the swimmer can answer while swimming or immediately at the wall:

- Did breathing remain comfortable, or did I delay it until urgent?
- Did taking a breath change my direction, kick width, or rhythm?
- Did I feel consistent pressure on the water, or repeated slipping?
- Did I start each repetition too fast?
- Did my cue remain manageable near the end?
- Did any movement hurt?
- Could I repeat this with similar quality after the intended rest?

Treat sensations as useful feedback, not proof of exact joint positions. “I felt my elbow high” does not verify the underwater catch. “The drill felt awkward” does not establish that it is wrong, but pain, distress, or lack of transfer are reasons to change it.

### Video collection

Ask permission from the facility and avoid recording other swimmers unnecessarily. Prefer an above-water camera on deck or a safely mounted underwater camera. Do not instruct a person to hold their breath on the pool floor, use weights to remain submerged, or obstruct an active lane to film.

Request short clips of normal swimming at a comfortable pace and, if appropriate, at the relevant faster pace. Include several complete cycles. Useful views are side above water, front or rear, and side underwater. A side view helps with breathing and timing; front/rear views help with alignment; underwater views reveal parts of the catch and kick hidden by the surface. See USMS underwater observation guidance (R10).

Ask for stroke, pace/effort, equipment, whether the clip was early or late in the session, and the athlete's question. If only still images are available, do not infer timing from a single frame. If the runtime cannot inspect video, state that and ask for frames or a coach's observations.

### Review video in passes

1. Watch at normal speed for rhythm and direction.
2. Watch head, shoulders, trunk, and hips together.
3. Review the breath and its effect on the next movement.
4. Inspect arms and legs only where the view supports it.
5. Compare several cycles and both sides.
6. Rewatch at normal speed before concluding.

Use timestamps where possible. Distinguish “visible,” “partly visible,” and “not visible.” Avoid precise joint-angle claims from distorted underwater footage. Do not assign a universal numerical technique score.

Give at most two priority observations at once, usually one. Describe the potential benefit, a short task, and the evidence that would support continuing it.

### Simple cue comparison

After warming up, swim two similar short repetitions normally, then two using a cue, with the same rest and intended effort. If the comparison is promising, repeat it on another day. For a stronger comparison, alternate the order next time.

Look for practical improvement without new problems. One faster repetition can reflect effort, learning the task, or timing noise. Do not claim a causal result from one pair.

## Freestyle guide

Use freestyle as the default conditioning stroke only after confirming it is appropriate and preferred. Read the USMS freestyle overview (R11) for individual variation; use the linked component guides when a specific issue needs more detail.

### Body line and rotation

Keep the head comfortable and the trunk travelling in one direction. Look generally down, adjusted to the swimmer's natural position, rather than lifting the face forward. Aim for legs that follow the body without a deep bend at the hips.

Rotate the shoulders and hips as part of the stroke while keeping the head comparatively steady between breaths. Do not force the swimmer to roll onto their side on every stroke or demand an exact rotation angle. Excessive roll can make breathing and the next catch harder.

Useful cue: **“Travel along one narrow track.”**

Self-check: Does the body remain directed down the lane when breathing? Video check: Do the hips swing sideways or the legs separate as the hand enters? These features warrant investigation; they do not prove weak abdominal muscles.

Reference: Freestyle body position and rotation (R12).

### Hand entry, catch, and pull

Enter in a comfortable line roughly in front of the shoulder. Avoid reaching across the head's centre line. Allow the arm to extend without aggressively locking the elbow or forcing the shoulder forward.

The catch prepares the hand and forearm to apply useful pressure. Bend and orient the arm so pressure acts mainly backward as the body travels forward. Do not prescribe a rigid elbow height, a forced early vertical forearm, or a deliberate S-shaped path for everyone.

Let the pull flow into the next phase instead of pausing with the hand at the thigh. Use “feel the forearm help move water backward” rather than “grab a fixed point”; water is not a stationary handle.

Reference: Freestyle pull guide (R13).

### Recovery and continuity

Recover the arm comfortably over the water and place the hand for the next stroke. A bent-elbow or straighter-arm recovery can work; select by comfort, speed, and entry quality. Do not confuse a high elbow during recovery with the underwater catch.

Avoid an exaggerated front glide that stalls the body between strokes. A longer-looking stroke is not automatically more efficient. At sprint pace, expect rhythm to differ from easy swimming.

Self-check: Can the swimmer recover without shoulder pinching or deliberately lifting the whole arm with tension? Does the entry become better when the recovery is relaxed? Use short ordinary swims to test this, not a requirement to match an elite swimmer's appearance.

### Breathing

Release air while the face is submerged so the breathing opportunity can be used mainly to inhale. Turn with the body rather than lifting the head forwards. Return the face smoothly without delaying the next useful arm movement.

A “one goggle near the water” cue can help limit head lifting, but it is not a strict anatomical target. The swimmer must actually obtain air. Do not insist on an extremely low breath that causes repeated water inhalation.

Breathing every two arm strokes is a valid choice. Practising the other side can develop flexibility, but bilateral skill does not require breathing every three strokes at all intensities. Alternate breathing sides by length if that works better. Never lengthen the breath interval as a toughness test.

Reference: Freestyle breathing guide (R14).

### Kick

Use a compact alternating kick with movement transmitted from the hips through relaxed knees and ankles. “From the hips” does not mean rigid knees. Excessive cycling, very wide kicks, or forcefully pointed toes can waste effort or feel uncomfortable.

Two-, four-, and six-beat patterns describe kicks per full arm cycle, not per hand entry. Different patterns can suit different speeds and swimmers. Do not demand a six-beat kick for every distance or remove the kick entirely from endurance swimming.

Useful cue: **“Keep the kick inside your body's track.”** If the legs split only during breathing, investigate the breath and alignment before adding harder kick sets.

Reference: Freestyle kick guide (R15).

### Fault investigation table

| Report or observation            | Investigate first                                            | Initial task                                                 | Evidence of improvement                            |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Breathlessness after one length  | Starting effort, exhalation, access to air, water confidence | Short easy repeats with comfortable breathing                | Less urgency at the same easy task                 |
| Head lifts for each breath       | Timing, roll, leading-hand pressure                          | Side-balance drill then ordinary swimming                    | Breath with less change in body line               |
| Hand crosses the centre line     | Entry and recovery from front view                           | Shoulder-track entries                                       | Straighter travel without forced width             |
| Large scissor kick               | Breath and balance, then leg control                         | Short drill/swim alternation                                 | Less leg separation during the breath              |
| Fast arms but little speed       | Entry, catch, timing, fatigue                                | Closed-fist contrast then normal hands                       | Better pressure and useful speed at similar effort |
| Long glide and repeated stall    | Over-reaching or delayed catch                               | Continuous-rhythm swimming                                   | Less dead time without frantic turnover            |
| Legs tire much earlier than arms | Kick intensity, ankle comfort, overall pace                  | Lower-effort compact kick in full stroke                     | More sustainable whole-stroke effort               |
| Shoulder discomfort              | Pain pattern, recent load, equipment                         | Stop provoking work; review with an appropriate professional | Do not use a faster time as clearance              |

### Learning sequence

For a learner: comfortable exhalation and recovery to support → short whole strokes with access to air → repeatable line and rhythm → catch refinement → longer repeats → short speed with retained breathing.

For an experienced swimmer: choose the observed limitation directly. Do not restart every athlete with beginner balance drills.

## Backstroke guide

Use backstroke to develop a separate skill, not as an automatically easy recovery stroke. Some swimmers find it demanding or uncomfortable. Read Swim England's backstroke guide (R16) and USMS backstroke technique (R17).

### Head and body position

Face upwards with a relaxed neck. Let the head remain relatively still while the trunk rotates. Seek a supported body position rather than holding the face high to keep it dry. Avoid forcing the lower back into a large arch to raise the hips.

Self-check: Does the head wobble with each arm? Is the neck tiring? Do the knees repeatedly rise high above the surface? Use these observations to choose one adjustment, not a checklist to tense against.

A small head-position experiment can help: compare two comfortable positions during easy short swims and observe the effect on the hips and effort. Do not exaggerate into painful neck positions.

### Arms and propulsion

Recover one arm above the body in a comfortable path. Enter approximately in line with that shoulder rather than behind the head. A little-finger-first orientation is a common cue, but do not twist the shoulder forcefully to achieve it.

Underwater, bend the arm to direct pressure towards the feet and finish into the next recovery. Avoid treating the arm as a rigid windmill underwater. Coordinate rotation with the arm action; keep the opposite shoulder free to recover.

Self-check: Is one entry crossing behind the head? Does the swimmer travel sideways? Observe from behind or in front before deciding which component caused the deviation.

### Kick, rhythm, and breathing

Use a narrow flutter kick with mobile ankles and enough knee bend for the feet to move effectively. Avoid a cycling action in which the knees rise while the feet mostly push downward.

Breathe regularly. Having the face out of the water does not guarantee the swimmer is breathing comfortably. Choose a natural pattern instead of holding the breath through several strokes.

Build arm rhythm and rotation together. Do not rotate the head to follow each hand or insist on rolling fully onto the side.

### Navigation and walls

Use fixed overhead references indoors when available. Outdoors, establish lane awareness without repeatedly lifting the head. Learn the meaning and location of backstroke flags, and practise a controlled approach with supervision before increasing speed.

Never assume a memorized stroke count is sufficient at every speed or in every pool. Counts change with pace, fatigue, and distance from the flags. Verify the approach and finish in the actual setting.

### Self-assessment and progression

| Observation                         | Practice choice                                | Check during whole stroke               |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Hips low and neck tense             | Comfortable back kicking with arms by sides    | Easier neck and more stable line        |
| Body stays flat while arms struggle | Short rotation practice then single-arm work   | Recovery becomes less forced            |
| Entry crosses behind head           | Single-arm entry focus                         | Entry stays on its shoulder track       |
| Knees cycle upwards                 | Gentle back kick with small range              | Feet contribute without large knee lift |
| Direction changes every stroke      | Review entry, kick symmetry, and head movement | Straighter travel over a short length   |
| Wall approach feels uncertain       | Slow approach practice with safe lane space    | Repeatable controlled arrival           |

Progress from supported confidence on the back to short kicking, controlled single-arm practice, alternating arms, and then longer repeats. Use the backstroke body-position drills (R18) and pull drills (R19) for demonstrations and alternatives. Do not prescribe advanced rotation drills merely because a list contains them.

## Breaststroke guide

Treat breaststroke as a timing and drag-management skill as well as a strength task. Read the USMS breaststroke overview (R20). Use full stroke with a normal breath each cycle for the basic learning task; avoid underwater pullout work as a prerequisite.

### Body position and breathing

Allow the upper body to rise enough for the breath, then direct it forward into a long position. Avoid holding the head high continuously or diving steeply downward after every breath.

Think of a cycle between breathing and moving forward through a narrow shape. The body need not remain perfectly flat at every instant. Excessive vertical motion, however, can make the swimmer spend energy rising and falling instead of travelling.

Useful cue: **“Breathe, then reach forward into the next movement.”**

Reference: Breaststroke body position (R21).

### Arm action

From the extended position, move the hands outward into a useful catch, then bring them inward and forward into recovery. Keep the action compact enough that recovery does not require dragging the arms back from the hips.

Avoid pulling the hands far behind the body during normal surface breaststroke. The underwater pullout is a separate skill with separate rules. Do not copy its long pull into each surface cycle.

Self-check: Does the swimmer pause with hands beneath the chest? Is the outward sweep extremely wide? Does a smaller, smoother action make the return forward easier?

Reference: Breaststroke pull (R22).

### Kick

Recover the heels towards the body without excessively driving the knees forwards. Turn the feet outward as comfortably available, then press water backward and bring the legs together into a long position.

The feet need a different orientation from the relaxed pointed position used during a flutter kick. Do not demand extreme turnout, force the knees apart, or stretch the ankle through pain. Individual hip, knee, and ankle structure affects the comfortable movement.

A gentle kick on the back can make the leg action easier to see. Keep it controlled and stop if it provokes knee discomfort.

Reference: Breaststroke kick (R23).

### Timing

Coordinate the breath and arm action, recover the hands forward, and deliver the kick into a lengthened body position. “Pull, breathe, kick, glide” can introduce the sequence, but real movements overlap; do not teach four rigid pauses.

Avoid a powerful kick while the hands and arms are still creating a large forward-facing obstacle. Likewise, do not rush into the next pull before benefiting from the kick.

The glide changes with speed. A long pause might help a slow learning drill but can stall faster swimming. Increase continuity for sprinting without losing the coordinated sequence.

Reference: Breaststroke timing (R24).

### Self-assessment and progression

| Observation                                      | Useful question                                    | First practice option                                       |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pull and kick seem to cancel each other          | Are the arms still returning when the kick drives? | Slow sequencing, then normal stroke                         |
| Heels recover with a large forward knee movement | Can the recovery be smaller and more comfortable?  | Gentle back kick, then short whole stroke                   |
| Little forward movement from the kick            | Are the feet orienting to push water backward?     | Supported kick rehearsal with a qualified teacher if needed |
| Large vertical bounce                            | Is the breath higher or later than necessary?      | Low-breath, forward-recovery focus                          |
| Long dead stop after each cycle                  | Is glide being held after speed has faded?         | Slightly earlier next cycle at the same easy effort         |
| Knee discomfort                                  | Does the kick reproduce it?                        | Stop that action and seek appropriate assessment            |

Build from comfortable kick and short coordinated strokes to repeatable 25s/50s, then longer work. Do not use breaststroke as the automatic substitute for a swimmer with an undiagnosed lower-limb problem.

For the two-hand touch and pullout, see Section 14. Retrieve current event rules before giving competition legality advice.

## Butterfly guide

Build butterfly with short, successful segments. Do not ask the athlete to grind through a long distance once the stroke has become a struggle to get the arms out. Read the USMS butterfly overview (R25) and its component guides.

### Body movement

Use a modest wave through the trunk and legs that supports forward travel. Avoid trying to create a dramatic chest dive or a large lower-back bend. The hands, breath, and kicks should help the swimmer return to a useful line near the surface.

Useful cue: **“Move forward over the water, not up over it.”** This is a movement intention, not a demand that the shoulders never rise.

Reference: Butterfly body position (R26).

### Arm action and recovery

Enter both hands comfortably in front of the shoulders, establish useful pressure with both arms, and let the finish lead into a relaxed forward recovery. Avoid throwing the hands high overhead or forcing a very narrow entry.

If the arms cannot clear comfortably, investigate speed, kick timing, breath timing, and fatigue before declaring that the swimmer lacks strength. Regress to a simpler task rather than repeatedly practising a failed recovery.

Reference: Butterfly pull (R27).

### Dolphin kick

Keep the legs acting together, with a movement that travels through the body and comfortable knee bend. Avoid a kick produced only by bending the knees while the trunk remains rigid. Equally, do not force a large spinal undulation.

Start with short surface kicking on the back or side if comfortable. Fins can make the rhythm easier to feel, but fin speed is not evidence of unassisted butterfly capacity.

Reference: Butterfly kick (R28).

### Timing and breathing

A common whole-stroke pattern uses two kicks per arm cycle: one around hand entry/extension and one around the end of the pull as the arms move into recovery. Use this as a rhythm guide rather than demanding an identical frame-by-frame pattern from everyone.

Take the breath as the upper body naturally rises through the pull, then return the face before the hands re-enter. Avoid leaving the head lifted through entry, which can disrupt the next cycle.

Breathe as needed. Every-cycle breathing can be appropriate. Do not prescribe alternate-cycle or no-breath butterfly as a default technique standard.

Reference: Butterfly timing (R29).

### Self-assessment and progression

| Observation                           | Investigate                                   | First adjustment                                     |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Hands land while face is still lifted | Breath timing                                 | Earlier return of the face on a short segment        |
| Arms drag through the surface         | Timing, fatigue, excessive vertical movement  | Fewer full strokes, more recovery, simpler drill     |
| One large kick but no second rhythm   | Relationship to arm entry and finish          | Single-arm drill with clear two-kick rhythm          |
| Deep dive after each entry            | Exaggerated chest press or late breath        | Smaller movement and forward direction               |
| Lower-back discomfort                 | Provoking movement and recent load            | Stop the provoking task; do not push more undulation |
| Good first few strokes then collapse  | Current repeat length exceeds useful capacity | End full stroke earlier and continue easy freestyle  |

Progression example: surface dolphin rhythm → single-arm butterfly → a few complete strokes → short full-stroke segments within 25 m → full 25 m repetitions with ample rest. Move on only when the current task is comfortable and coordinated.

A mixed length such as several butterfly strokes followed by easy freestyle is a skill exercise. It is not a legal continuous butterfly benchmark or evidence that a longer butterfly race is ready.

## Starts, turns, streamlines, and finishes

### General wall skills

Train the approach, contact or rotation, foot placement, push, streamline, and transition as linked parts. Better wall execution can improve pool times without a change in surface-swimming speed; label that progress accurately.

Use a normal comfortable push-off and early breakout for ordinary self-coached practice. Underwater skill is not measured by how long the swimmer can avoid breathing.

Read the USMS turns guide (R31) for demonstrations. Its coaching material does not replace the applicable competition rulebook.

### Freestyle tumble turn

For a swimmer already comfortable somersaulting with instruction, refine approach distance, a compact turn, controlled foot placement, and rotation into a useful exit. Exhale gently through the nose as appropriate to avoid water entry; breathe normally before and after the skill.

If the swimmer cannot reliably orient or reach the wall, use a controlled open turn and seek in-person teaching. Do not prescribe high-speed tumble turns as the solution to discomfort in the water.

### Backstroke turn and finish

Teach lane navigation and a controlled wall approach before a fast rollover turn. Competition backstroke turns and finishes have precise requirements, including what actions are permitted after leaving the back position. Retrieve the current applicable rules when advising on legality rather than reconstructing them from memory.

For an ordinary backstroke finish, arrive on the back with control and protect the head from the wall. Do not instruct a desperate last-second turn of the body to reach farther.

### Breaststroke and butterfly open turns

For standard competition practice, approach to a simultaneous two-hand touch, gather the legs, turn, place the feet, and push away in the required orientation. Practise slowly enough to coordinate it. Do not slide along the wall or pull on the gutter as a substitute for a repeatable turn.

Use the exact governing rules for hand separation, orientation, and subsequent underwater actions. A practice drill may intentionally differ from legal stroke, so label it.

### Breaststroke pullout

Treat the pullout as an optional advanced skill. Establish a comfortable surface stroke and competent wall skills first. It combines an underwater arm action, permitted kick actions, and a timely return to surface swimming, with precise competition restrictions.

Use qualified instruction for learning it. Retrieve current regulations before specifying exact legality. Do not prescribe longer pullouts as a breathing-capacity challenge or apply a generic 15 m rule to breaststroke.

### Dive starts

Default to push starts unless the swimmer has appropriate instruction and the facility permits diving in the designated area. Block starts require suitable depth, clearance, and supervision according to facility rules. Do not infer safety from the athlete's strength or swimming speed.

If starts become a goal, refer to qualified on-deck coaching. The assistant can help organize feedback and compare consistent video/timing; text alone should not substitute for supervised acquisition.

### Competition rules

Use World Aquatics' rules hub (R32) and current competition regulations (R33), then the relevant national or Masters rules. The regulations landing page reviewed for this edition listed a February 2026 version; recheck for the event date.

Do not assume all Masters jurisdictions use identical stroke exceptions. Do not prescribe a maximum permitted underwater distance as a target. A legal upper limit is not an instruction to remain submerged that far.

For individual medley, the usual order is butterfly, backstroke, breaststroke, freestyle. For medley relay, the usual order is backstroke, breaststroke, butterfly, freestyle. Verify event-specific transition requirements before coaching a competition turn.
